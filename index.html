OUR STARTING POINT

A horizontal, rigid circular pipe. An incompressible Newtonian fluid with constant properties and no slip. Steady, fully developed laminar flow.

What does “fully developed” mean?

The velocity profile no longer changes along the pipe. Here, L is a section already fully developed, and ΔP is the pressure drop across that section. Steady flow alone does not imply zero acceleration; fully developed axial flow makes axial acceleration zero here. The inlet region is outside this model.

05 / OPTIONAL EXTENSION

When does this model apply?Explore flow regime and entrance development after the main lesson.+

The explorer assumes laminar, fully developed flow. It does not solve turbulence or an inlet developing region. A low Reynolds number does not, by itself, establish fully developed flow.

Re = ρūD/μ, with D = 2R. Common textbook regime boundaries are approximate; inlet disturbances affect transition. An entrance estimate such as Lₑ ≈ 0.06 Re D is a rough laminar-flow correlation, especially limited at very low Re. It is not a guarantee of development.

L in this explorer is already downstream of development. Do not compare it with Lₑ to certify the model: the upstream entrance distance is a separate quantity. Entrance pressure loss is not included.

The entrance estimate is intentionally omitted from the calculation at very low Re or outside the nominal laminar range. This section provides context, not a developing-flow simulation.

A SECOND QUESTION / MICROFLUIDIC MIXING

Flow moves fluid.
Diffusion mixes it.

Two streams can travel side by side without becoming uniform. Compare an analytical solution with a numerical model, then investigate how changing the diffusion distance changes mixing.

Open the mixing lab ↗

FROM MOMENTUM TO MASS TRANSFER

Conceptual sketch, not simulation output. Pressure determines motion; concentration gradients drive molecular diffusion.

Connection to the first tab: the force balance and viscosity law describe momentum transport. Mixing adds a species balance and molecular diffusivity Dm in m²/s. Dm is not viscosity μ and is not pipe diameter.

This lab uses independent channel inputs. It does not apply the circular-pipe formula to a rectangular microchannel.

01 / THE ANALYTICAL APPROACH

Compare the time available
with the distance to diffuse.

Use a small model to understand the trend.
Use a larger model when geometry matters.

Time in the channel

tress = L / Um / (m/s) = s

Slower mean speed U or a longer channel L gives diffusion more time. This assumes a single representative transit speed.

A diffusion timescale

tD ∼ ℓ² / Dmm² / (m²/s) = s

Reducing a diffusion length scale ℓ by half reduces this timescale by four. This scaling is not a specific “fully mixed” threshold.

Advection vs. diffusion

PeW = UW / Dm(m/s) · m / (m²/s) = 1

Pe compares transport using channel width W. A large Pe does not guarantee good mixing; residence time and transverse geometry also matter.

From the species balance to an analytical solution

For a passive, dilute species with constant diffusivity and no reaction, the steady advection–diffusion equation is u · ∇c = Dm∇²c. Both sides have units of concentration per second.

Use a uniform representative axial speed U, omit axial diffusion, and resolve only transverse position y. Then U ∂c/∂x = Dm ∂²c/∂y². Substituting t = x/U produces the 1D diffusion equation ∂c/∂t = Dm ∂²c/∂y².

For the baseline, c = 0 on the left half of a channel of width W, and c = 1 on the right. At the walls, ∂c/∂y = 0: solute cannot leave the channel. c is concentration divided by the solute-rich inlet concentration, so it is dimensionless. The final uniform value is 0.5.

c(y,t) = ½ − (2/π) Σm=1,3,5,… [sin(mπ/2)/m] cos(mπy/W) exp[−m²π²Dmt/W²]

The cosine series satisfies the no-flux walls. Every concentration mode decays, with finer spatial variations decaying faster. The browser evaluates a truncated series and averages it over the same cells as the numerical method.

Model boundary: uniform axial speed is a transport approximation, not the parabolic no-slip profile from the first tab. This lab does not resolve the depth, spatially varying residence time, secondary flows, reactions, or wall adsorption. When axial diffusion is important, this downstream-marching reduction is unsuitable.

How is “mixed” measured?

We use M = 1 − σ / 0.5 = 1 − 2σ, where σ is the standard deviation of normalized concentration across the width. Equal segregated streams give M = 0; uniform c = 0.5 gives M = 1. This is a spatial uniformity metric, not a reaction yield or a universal mixing specification. “90%” means M ≥ 0.9 by this definition.

The interface count n replaces the Fourier wavenumber mπ/W with mnπ/W, giving the idealized timescale improvement 1/n². It does not predict the performance or pressure cost of a particular mixer.

03 / THE COMPUTATIONAL APPROACH

A numerical result
needs a numerical check.

Discretize the same model.
Compare before adding complexity.

What the solver actually does

  1. Divide W into equal cells and store their mean concentrations.
  2. Approximate diffusion fluxes between neighboring cells; set the flux at both walls to zero.
  3. Advance in residence time using backward Euler and a tridiagonal linear solve.
  4. Compare outlet cells to averages of the analytical solution.
(cⱼk+1 − cⱼk)/Δt = Dₘ(cⱼ₊₁k+1 − 2cⱼk+1 + cⱼ₋₁k+1)/Δy²

Interior-cell formula; boundary cells use a zero-flux face. Spatial diffusion is second-order accurate for smooth solutions; backward Euler is first-order in time. Stability alone does not establish accuracy.

Try a refinement study

Compare errors at successive resolutions. For a separated check, vary only cells or only time steps.

A very small outlet error after nearly complete mixing is an easy test. Also inspect partially mixed cases, conservation, bounds, and refinement.

What would a full CFD study add?

Build the actual 2D/3D geometry, solve incompressible continuity and momentum for u and pressure, then solve species transport using that velocity. Apply inlet flow/concentration and no-slip/impermeable-wall conditions; define an outlet condition consistent with the flow and transport regime. Compare cross-section concentration variance, pressure drop, residence time, and wall shear.

Verify the baseline against an analytical solution; check mesh and time-step convergence, numerical diffusion, mass conservation, and boundary-condition sensitivity. Validate against measured concentration fields or mixing data when available. Herringbone features and secondary circulation generally require a 3D model. No such CFD or experimental validation is claimed by this browser lab.

04 / CHOOSE A MIXING STRATEGY

Give diffusion less distance
or more time.

Compare mechanisms and tradeoffs.
A curved channel is not automatically a good mixer.

PASSIVE / THE BASELINE

Longer residence time

Increase length or lower speed so diffusion can act for longer. At a given throughput, longer channels generally require more driving pressure. Lowering speed also lowers throughput for fixed cross-sectional area.

Study it: compare tres with a defined mixing target, then verify the outlet concentration profile.

PASSIVE / SHORTER DISTANCES

Hydrodynamic focusing

Sheath streams narrow a central sample stream, reducing the distance for molecular diffusion. Flow-rate ratios control the focused stream. NIST uses this approach in microfluidic liposome formation.

Study it: estimate the focused width, then solve the resulting concentration geometry. The equal-volume baseline here does not represent a focused central stream.

NIST: microfluidic synthesis ↗
PASSIVE / LAMELLATION

Split and recombine

Repeatedly split and rearrange streams to form thinner alternating layers. Diffusion then acts over shorter distances. Fabrication, flow distribution, and added pressure loss must also be considered.

Study it: use the layering control as an ideal inlet comparison. A real device must generate those layers; the control does not model splitting or recombination.

PASSIVE / TRANSVERSE MOTION

Herringbone & serpentine channels

Herringbone grooves can stretch and fold fluid through transverse motion, even while flow remains laminar. A serpentine may simply add residence time; curvature-driven secondary motion depends on geometry and flow conditions.

Study it: resolve the actual velocity field and species transport. Do not assign a generic “mixing improvement” to every bend.

Stroock et al.: chaotic mixer for microchannels ↗
ACTIVE / EXTERNAL ENERGY

Acoustic or electric forcing

External fields can create motion that moves material across streamlines. Performance depends on the actuation, fluid properties, geometry, and operating conditions.

Study it: couple the relevant forcing to flow and transport, and check energy input, heating, and sample compatibility. These mechanisms are outside this reduced solver.

YOUR ENGINEERING QUESTION

Which design gives enough mixing at an acceptable pressure cost?

First compare straight-channel and ideal-layering results at equal W, U, L, and Dₘ. Then identify which omitted physics a real design would need. Mixing index alone does not select a device.
